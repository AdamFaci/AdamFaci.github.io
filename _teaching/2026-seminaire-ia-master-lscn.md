Research seminar on AI for the LSCN master's programme, taught with Irène Langlet.

The seminar is built on a single wager: that a literal description of what the machine computes is more useful to a humanities researcher than a personifying metaphor — not because metaphors are inelegant, but because they generate false predictions, and a false prediction wastes time.

Outline
======

**1. Define.** What the word "AI" covers: a discipline named in 1956, dozens of unrelated technical families, a handful of commercial products, and — since 2024 — a distinct legal definition in the European AI Act. Most confused discussions about "AI" turn out to be a sentence in which one of those senses is used and another is heard. The session then works down the nested set (machine learning, neural networks, deep learning, transformers, generative language models), separates symbolic AI (*rules are written*) from learned AI (*numbers are adjusted*), and situates the tasks already long-established in the humanities — HTR, lemmatisation, named entity recognition, alignment, stylistic measurement — of which only the last, generation, is what the newspapers discuss.

**2. Open.** From "counting words" (1949) to "producing a commentary": what a model is, formally — a fixed sequence of operations with several billion adjustable parameters, and nothing else in the file but those numbers and the description of the computation. A translation table then pairs each common phrase ("it understands", "it learns", "it knows", "it hallucinates", "it lies", "it thinks") with what is actually computed and with the specific wrong prediction the metaphor produces.

**3. Use.** Five working rules, each deduced from the preceding section rather than from a moral position.
