I am a postdoctoral researcher at Huma-Num (UAR 3598, CNRS), the French national research infrastructure for the digital humanities, where I work in the HN Lab on large language models and knowledge graphs for the social sciences and humanities.

I hold a PhD in Artificial Intelligence from Sorbonne Université (LIP6, 2022), on the representation, simulation and mining of knowledge in the conceptual graph formalism. Since then my work has moved from purely symbolic knowledge representation towards neuro-symbolic and interdisciplinary methods: how generative models can be made controllable, documentable and interpretable enough to serve as research instruments for historians, literary scholars, linguists and social scientists — and how the epistemological assumptions built into those models reshape the disciplines that adopt them.

Research interests

Generation

Retrieval-augmented generation and scholarly agents

Making model outputs traceable back to a controlled corpus, and routing a research question to an explicit, inspectable plan rather than to a single opaque generation. I lead the ReSearch SSH use case of the European project LLMs4EU.

Knowledge

Knowledge graphs and neuro-symbolic methods

Conceptual graphs, ontologies, pattern mining, and the combination of symbolic structure with learned representations — including ontological “lenses” placed over conversational agents.

Vision

Computer vision for art history and photojournalism

Frugal segmentation and clustering pipelines for large image corpora, and the epistemological cost of reducing an expert description to a single annotation label. See SegmentART.

Evaluation

Benchmarking, bias and research ethics

Evaluating language models on scholarly SSH literature, measuring identity bias in generated peer review, and turning those findings into guidelines SSH communities can apply.

Epistemology

Socio-history of AI in the humanities, and research practices

How AI methods, and media discourse about them, have circulated through French digital humanities; how to compose with new methods from an SSH field’s point of view; and how applying AI methods to SSH research yields insights back into computer science.

I also teach and run training sessions on AI for humanities audiences, from master's seminars to CNRS national training schools. I take that teaching to be part of the research: the classroom is where the methodological assumptions get tested.
